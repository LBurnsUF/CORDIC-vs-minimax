Fixed-point trigonometry appears in motor control loops, sensor fusion pipelines, and 
signal processing  routines across the embedded domain. The choice of algorithm has 
real consequences for execution time, code size, and numerical accuracy, all of which 
are tightly constrained on a part with 128 kilobytes of flash and a 32 MHz clock.

A floating-point unit makes trigonometry trivial. Issue a single instruction, receive a
result in IEEE 754 format, move on.  The ATxmega128A1U, however, is an 8-bit AVR 
microcontroller with no FPU, no barrel shifter, and no division hardware. 
What it does have is an 8-by-8 hardware multiplier. 
So, given only addition, subtraction, bitwise shifts, and that single multiply instruction,
how do you compute sine and cosine?

The unit circle is the place to start. A vector of unit length, rotated to some angle
theta, has projections onto the x and y axes equal to cosine and sine. As the vector sweeps 
through a full rotation, the vertical projection traces out the familiar sine wave. 
Every algorithm we look at here is, at its core, trying to compute those two projections 
using nothing but integer arithmetic.


II. FIXED-POINT REPRESENTATION

Before looking at any algorithm, lets cover the number format. Without floating-point 
hardware, fractional values are encoded in fixed-point format: an ordinary integer 
whose bits are interpreted as having an implicit binary point. A 16-bit register with 
14 fractional bits, denoted Q1.14, can represent values from negative two to just 
under two, with a resolution of roughly 0.00006 per step.

As the number of fractional bits decreases, the set of representable values coarsens. 
This quantization sets a hard floor. No algorithm can produce accuracy finer than the
format itself allows.

Fixed-point multiplication is worth pausing on. The product of two Q1.14 values
requires a 28-bit intermediate result before truncation back to 16 bits. On an 8-bit
AVR, that 16-by-16 multiply decomposes into four hardware MUL instructions plus a
chain of additions. At 32-bit width, the decomposition requires sixteen MUL instructions. 
The cost of multiplication grows quadratically with the number of limbs.


III. THE LOOKUP TABLE

The most direct approach is also the most memory-intensive. Precompute sine values
at uniform angular intervals, store them in flash, and interpolate between neighbors
at runtime. This works, and it is widely used, but it has two costs: the table itself
consumes flash in proportion to the desired angular resolution, and the interpolation
between entries still requires a multiply and a divide. On a part where both
operations are expensive, the lookup table is viable but not free.


IV. CORDIC

CORDIC, the Coordinate Rotation Digital Computer, was developed by Jack Volder in 1959 
for a navigation computer that could not multiply. The core insight is as follows: A 
rotation  by an arbitrary angle can be decomposed into a sequence of successively smaller 
rotations, each  chosen so that the tangent of the rotation angle is an exact power of two. 
That constraint transforms what would ordinarily be a multiply into a bit shift.

The algorithm initializes a vector along the x-axis and iteratively rotates it toward 
the target angle. At each step, the sign of the residual angle determines the direction 
of the next rotation: 
If the residual is still positive, rotate forward. 
if it has overshot, rotate back. 

First, accuracy improves by approximately one bit per iteration. The convergence is
linear, and the number of useful iterations is bounded by the number of fractional
bits in the fixed-point format.

Second, each pseudo-rotation slightly stretches the vector. The stretch factor for
iteration i is the square root of one plus two to the negative two-i, and the product
of all these factors across all iterations is a constant K, approximately 1.647. Here
is what makes that useful: K does not depend on the target angle. Its reciprocal can
be applied once as a prescale of the initial x value, eliminating any per-iteration
multiply. The entire loop body is nothing but shifts and adds.

One caveat that is frequently omitted: CORDIC still requires a small arctangent table, 
one entry per iteration, stored in program memory. The table is far smaller than a 
full sine lookup table though, as its length equals the number of fractional bits.


V. POLYNOMIAL APPROXIMATION

The alternative to iterative rotation is to approximate sine and cosine directly with
a polynomial. The mathematical foundation for this is the Taylor series. Sine is an
analytic function, which means that near any point, it is exactly equal to an infinite
sum of terms built from its own derivatives at that point.

Each term involves a higher power of x divided by a factorial. The series is infinite
and converges everywhere, but for computation, we truncate it to a finite number of
terms. The truncated polynomial is an approximation, but the approximation can be made 
arbitrarily good for values near the expansion point by adding more terms.

There is a practical constraint, though. Polynomials approximate well only on a
bounded interval, and the Taylor series converges fastest near its expansion point.
But angles can span zero to two pi. This is where argument reduction comes in.
Sine and cosine have symmetries that allow any angle to be folded into the first 
octant, the interval from zero to pi/4. Restricting the domain means fewer terms 
are needed for a given accuracy.

With the domain restricted, the polynomial can be evaluated efficiently using
Horner's method. The naive approach computes each power of x independently, which
wastes multiplications. Horner restructures the polynomial to nest the operations:

  x * (1 + x^2 * (-1/6 + x^2 * (1/120 + ...)))

Evaluated from the inside out, this requires exactly one multiply and one add per
term. For a degree-n polynomial, that is n multiplies, optimal for any architecture
with a fast multiplier.

Now, there are two ways to choose the polynomial's coefficients, and the difference
between them is consequential.

Taylor coefficients come directly from the series expansion. They are closed-form
expressions involving factorials: 1, -1/6, 1/120, and so on. They are easy to compute
by hand and easy to verify. But they are optimized for accuracy at the origin,and 
the approximation deteriorates as you move away from it.

Minimax coefficients are constructed differently. Instead of anchoring accuracy at
the origin, the Remez exchange algorithm finds coefficients that minimize the
worst-case error across the entire interval. The resulting error curve does not ramp
up at the edges. Instead, it equioscillates: the error ripples uniformly across the
interval, never exceeding a certain bound. This is a consequence of the
Chebyshev equioscillation theorem, which guarantees that the minimax polynomial
is optimal in the max-norm sense.

For a given number of terms, the minimax polynomial has a strictly smaller worst-case 
error than the Taylor polynomial. Equivalently, it reaches a given accuracy target with 
fewer terms. Fewer terms means fewer multiplies at runtime.

The tradeoff is that minimax coefficients are not closed-form. They must be computed 
offline by an iterative numerical algorithm, the Remez Algorithm. Once computed, they 
are just constants embedded in the source code, no different from Taylor coefficients.

Neither variant requires a lookup table. No angle table, no precomputed sine values.
The coefficients are inline constants, and evaluation is a fixed sequence of multiplies
and adds.


VI. IMPLEMENTATION
The fixed-point format is parameterized at compile time: TOTAL_BITS selects the
backing integer type, and FRAC_BITS determines the binary point position. A
host-side Python script generates the appropriate constants for each configuration.

The CORDIC implementation is a straightforward loop. The initial vector is set to
(1/K, 0), the residual angle z is initialized to the input, and each iteration applies 
a conditional shift-add update to x, y, and z. 

The polynomial implementation uses Horner evaluation over the squared argument.
Sine uses an odd-polynomial form, cosine an even-polynomial form. The fixed-point
multiply is inlined.

One detail of implementation correctness that is easy to get wrong: the gain constant
K must be computed for the exact number of iterations the firmware performs. The
commonly cited asymptotic value of 1/K converges only as iterations approach infinity. 
Using it for a finite iteration count introduces a systematic bias that is entirely avoidable.


VII. MEASURED COMPARISON

Three fixed-point configurations are measured on hardware: Q1.6 at 8-bit, Q1.14 at
16-bit, and Q1.30 at 32-bit. These correspond to limb counts of one, two, and four.
Cycle counts are captured via the ATxmega's TCC0 timer in stopwatch mode, with a
width-dependent prescaler and a K-loop of 100 iterations to reduce quantization
error.

The results are presented in four plots. Consider each in turn.

Cycles versus width shows the total cost per call. Minimax is fastest at every width.
At 8-bit, where the hardware MUL handles the entire fixed-point multiply in one
instruction, the polynomial methods run at roughly half the cost of CORDIC. At
16-bit, both CORDIC and Taylor converge to around 1500 cycles, while minimax is 
still quicker at 1200. By 32-bit, CORDIC is slowest.

Accuracy versus fractional bits shows the maximum error in LSBs. Both polynomial
methods land at plus or minus one LSB across all widths, limited only by the
fixed-point truncation itself. CORDIC plateaus at four to five LSB,  a consequence
of accumulated rounding through the shift-add iterations.

Per-operation cost breaks the total into its components. Individual CORDIC
iterations are cheaper at all widths, since an iteration is just shifts and 
adds, while a polynomial term involves a multiply. By 32-bit, polynomial terms 
have become expensive due to the costly 64-bit intermediate multiply. 

CORDIC's total cost is still higher, and the operation count plot shows why:
CORDIC iterations scale linearly with precision, while Polynomial term
count grows much more slowly The iteration count keeps CORDIC's total cost high.

VIII. WHAT THIS TELLS US

These results are specific to the ATxmega128A1U, and that specificity is the point.
CORDIC was designed for hardware that could only shift and add. On such a machine,
every multiply in a polynomial evaluation would itself become an iterative shift-add
loop, and CORDIC's avoidance of multiplication would be a real advantage. The
ATxmega is not that machine. Its hardware MUL instruction executes in two clock
cycles, making polynomial evaluation efficient in a way that was simply not possible
on the hardware CORDIC was designed for.

That does not make CORDIC a poor algorithm. It makes it a solution to a problem the
ATxmega does not have. On an older microcontroller that lacks MUL entirely, you 
would expect the ranking to look quite different. The value of CORDIC is architectural: 
it trades away the need for multiplication, which is only advantageous when 
multiplication is expensive.

Among the polynomial methods, minimax is the stronger choice for production code.
It achieves equivalent or better accuracy with fewer terms. Taylor's advantage is 
purely pedagogical, it is easier to follow, and its coefficients come from a 
formula you can derive on a whiteboard. The minimax coefficients require an
offline computation via the Remez exchange algorithm, but once computed, they
produce a strictly better approximation.

The broader observation is that algorithm selection cannot be separated from
hardware context. The same mathematical operation admits fundamentally different 
optimal strategies depending on what the underlying silicon can do quickly. 
Understanding that relationship between algorithm and architecture is a core skill 
of embedded systems engineering.
